A DEFINITE VALUE

A bit has a value: 0 or 1.

A classical bit is a two-value record. Flipping it changes 0 to 1, or 1 to 0. Reading this ideal bit gives its current value.

A classical bit can also be random or unknown to us. Randomness alone is not what makes a qubit different.

AMPLITUDE + RELATIVE PHASE

More than a hidden 0 or 1.

A qubit is a two-level quantum system. Its state has an amplitude for each possible Z result. Squaring each amplitude’s magnitude gives that result’s probability.

Here the two amplitudes have equal size. Reverse one arrow: the Z probabilities stay 50/50, but the relative phase changes.

These arrows represent two amplitudes of one qubit, not two particles. They show just the two phases used in our A/B experiment; general amplitudes can be complex.

ONE TRIAL, ONE RESULT

You do not read every possibility.

A measurement gives one outcome. For A and B, Z gives a random 0 or 1 with equal probability. Choosing X instead reveals their phase difference: the two preparations give opposite, certain results.

Every click here prepares a fresh qubit. It is not repeatedly measuring the same unchanged qubit.

The next fresh trial can give 0 or 1.

The main experiment compares both preparations side by side. These buttons select its direction; changing direction clears old statistics.

So where can a quantum advantage come from?

With many qubits, a circuit transforms amplitudes for many possible bit strings. Useful quantum algorithms arrange interference so some amplitudes reinforce and others cancel. A measurement still returns one bit string, not all the answers. Speedups depend on the algorithm and problem; this one-qubit experiment does not demonstrate a computational speedup.
